# LiveMathematicianBench

Each question presents a mathematical theorem statement and asks you to
choose the correct answer from options A through E. Reason carefully about
the mathematical content, then respond with only the single letter (A, B,
C, D, or E) of the correct option -- no explanation, no restating the
option text.

## Insights

- Prefer the logically weakest option that fully satisfies the required conclusion, avoiding assumptions of uniqueness, exact structure, or additional properties unless strictly implied; when evaluating existence results or upper bounds, favor the most technically precise claim that does not impose extra uniformity, canonicality, or structural constraints (e.g., independence of parameters, joint continuity, fixed memory size, uniform-in-time bounds) unless explicitly proven or required, and recognize that for bounds of the form \( \mu(\Phi(t)(A)) \leq \mu(A)^p \exp(C(1+|t|)^q) \), larger \( p \) (closer to or greater than 1) strengthens the bound for small \( \mu(A) \), while smaller \( q \) implies slower growth and thus a stronger long-term estimate.
- When multiple options appear similar, prioritize the one that correctly accounts for uniformity in constants, boundary conditions, compatibility, or stochastic adaptivity as required by the problem setting, and verify that the proposed solution space or estimate is consistent with the known scaling, embedding, or energy estimates implied by the PDE or stochastic dynamics.
- Be cautious of options that claim uniform-in-time results (e.g., supremum over n ∈ ℕ) unless the system has strong dissipativity or Lyapunov structure explicitly ensuring stability; otherwise, finite-time or exponentially weighted bounds may be more accurate.
- Prefer the option whose logical structure and quantifiers are necessary and sufficient for the conclusion; avoid selecting statements that are true but logically too weak, even if they appear similar or are satisfied in the given setting, especially when tighter bounds, stronger convergence, or exact structural conditions are required. When evaluating stability, shadowing, or regularity properties in dynamical, geometric, or analytic settings, recognize that the presence of neutral modes (e.g., eigenvalues on the unit circle, zero Lyapunov exponents, or characteristic directions) does not automatically destroy the property if those modes are structurally benign (e.g., semisimple, non-resonant, or uniformly controlled); conversely, absence of such modes is sufficient but not necessary unless the system lacks symmetry or integrability. Prefer options that provide a universal or canonical characterization (e.g., free object, initial object, exact classification) when such a characterization is known to hold, as these are typically the strongest correct statements; when a problem asks for a complete classification, correspondence, or characterization, favor the option that fully specifies the structure (e.g., as a span, direct sum, or bijective correspondence) rather than a proper subset or quotient; and be cautious of options that omit required invariance, completeness, or structural constraints (e.g., G-invariance, full eigenspace, integrability) when the mathematical context implies these are necessary for correctness.
- Prefer the logically weakest option that fully satisfies the required conclusion, favoring the minimal or optimal parameter, constant, or structural condition (e.g., smallest K, tightest bound, weakest algebraic requirement) when multiple options describe equivalent or nested conditions, and select meta-options indicating a stronger result can be proven if domain knowledge confirms it, even if explicit options are technically correct but suboptimal.
- Avoid selecting options that specify exact constants, functional forms, or structural details (e.g., precise coefficients, explicit denominators, exact eigenvalue counts) unless they are rigorously implied by the given conditions; prefer options that match the level of precision supported by the theory, especially when asymptotic, qualitative, or existential conclusions are the strongest justifiable claims.
- When a meta-option indicates that a stronger result than any explicitly listed can be proven, select it if domain knowledge confirms that known theorems or methods imply such an improvement, as this represents the strongest valid consequence even when the given options are technically correct but suboptimal.
- Avoid selecting options that include specific quantitative refinements (e.g., asymptotic expansions, optimality conditions, or restricted parameter sets) or unjustified upgrades in meta-level strength (e.g., residual instead of dense, exact thresholds, or structural implications without proof) unless strictly implied by the given assumptions; prefer existence or qualitative results, and the weakest meta-level claim (e.g., dense existence, non-sharp bounds), that still establishes the conclusion.
- When special structural assumptions are present (e.g., non-uniruledness, thickness bounds, complete invariance, or critical value confinement), verify whether they enable a stronger conclusion than generic existence or qualitative results, as such assumptions often imply precise structural or global consequences beyond local or generic behavior.
- Avoid selecting options that combine multiple independent strengthenings (e.g., infinitude, irreducibility, additional structural constraints, or two-sided quantitative bounds) unless all are strictly implied; prefer the option that is minimally sufficient, even if other options appear more informative or comprehensive.
- When a conclusion involves a limit or bound that must hold uniformly or asymptotically across a family of parameters (e.g., prime divisors, frequency modes, or spatial scales), verify that the condition is required to hold for all members of the family, not just the extremal or dominant one; prefer statements with universal quantification over such parameters unless the theory explicitly allows reduction to a single representative.
- Avoid selecting options that claim improved constants, asymptotic coefficients, or functional forms (e.g., tighter bounds with enhanced constants or exact expressions) unless they are rigorously derived from the given assumptions or established theorems; prefer the option that matches the proven asymptotic order or constant without enhancement, especially when the improvement is not uniformly valid or depends on unverified conditions.
